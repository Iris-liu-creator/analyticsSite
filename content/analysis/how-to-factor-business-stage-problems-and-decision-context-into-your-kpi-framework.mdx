> Core idea: the right KPI is not the metric that looks important. It is the metric that helps the business make the right decision at its current stage, with its current constraints, without creating the wrong behaviour.

A KPI is never just a number on a dashboard.

Once a metric is reviewed by leadership, discussed in meetings, tied to incentives, or used to judge performance, it starts shaping behaviour. People will naturally pay attention to it, optimise for it, defend it, and sometimes game it.

That is why KPI design deserves more thought than simply asking, “What should we measure?”

The better question is: what decision are we trying to improve, and what behaviour do we want this KPI to encourage?

A good KPI does more than describe performance. It helps the business focus on the right problem, make better trade-offs, and act with more confidence.

## 1. Start with the decision, not the dashboard

Many KPI discussions begin with a list of metrics: revenue, margin, leads, conversion, utilisation, churn, forecast accuracy, customer satisfaction.

All of these can be useful, but none of them are automatically the right KPI. The starting point should be the decision the business needs to make.

This changes the way we think about reporting. A sales dashboard should not simply show whether revenue is up or down. It should help leaders understand whether the sales engine is healthy, where deals are getting stuck, and whether the forecast is believable.

A marketing dashboard should not only show lead volume. It should help the business understand whether marketing is creating commercial opportunities or just activity.

A professional services dashboard should not only show hours worked. It should help leaders understand whether delivery effort is converting into profitable, timely, high-quality customer outcomes.

The KPI should serve the decision. Otherwise, the dashboard becomes a polished reporting surface with limited business value.

## 2. Match KPIs to the stage of the business

The right KPI also depends on the maturity of the business. A startup, a fast-scaling company, and a mature enterprise should not manage performance in exactly the same way.

### Early stage: prove the model

At an early stage, the business is usually still testing whether the product, market, pricing, and operating model work. The focus is less about perfect governance and more about learning quickly.

At this stage, overly complex KPI frameworks can slow the business down. The goal is to identify whether the business is moving in the right direction.

For example, if a SaaS company is still proving product-market fit, tracking total revenue alone is not enough. The company also needs to know whether customers are activating, using the product, and staying after the first few months. Revenue may look promising in the short term, but weak usage and early churn may tell a different story.

### Growth stage: build repeatability

Once the business has traction, the focus shifts to repeatability. Can the company acquire customers consistently? Can sales convert pipeline reliably? Can delivery keep up with growth? Can customer success retain and expand accounts?

At this stage, a business may celebrate rapid sales growth, but the more important question is whether the growth is healthy.

If sales is growing because the team is discounting heavily or selling poor-fit deals that are expensive to serve, the headline growth may hide future margin pressure. That is why strong KPI design often pairs an outcome metric with a quality or sustainability metric.

### Scale stage: improve control and accountability

As the business scales, decision makers usually need more discipline around process, ownership, and predictability.

At this stage, “more reporting” is not always the answer. The business needs clearer definitions, stronger ownership, and a rhythm for action.

For example, if the sales forecast is frequently missed, simply showing forecast numbers is not enough. The business may need to track close-date pushes, stage ageing, stale opportunities, next-step completeness, and forecast changes by week.

These KPIs reveal whether forecast risk is coming from deal quality, pipeline hygiene, rep behaviour, or market conditions.

### Mature stage: optimise and detect weak signals

In mature businesses, the challenge is often not visibility, but focus. There may already be many dashboards, metrics, reports, and review packs. The risk is that leaders spend time looking at numbers without seeing the early signals that matter.

## 3. Design KPIs around the current business problem

The same strategic goal can require very different KPIs depending on the current challenge. Take a common goal: increase revenue. The right KPI depends on what is stopping revenue growth.

This is why generic KPI lists are dangerous. A business with a pipeline generation problem does not need the same KPI focus as a business with a conversion problem. A business with high churn does not need the same leadership conversation as a business with low brand awareness.

For example, a marketing team may proudly report a 40% increase in leads. But if the sales team rejects most of those leads, the business problem is not lead volume. The real issue is conversion quality.

## 4. Balance descriptive, diagnostic, and forward-looking KPIs

A KPI framework should not only answer “What happened?” It should also help explain “Why did it happen?” and “What might happen next?”

    <thead className="bg-slate-50 text-xs uppercase tracking-[0.14em] text-slate-500">

      <tr><td className="px-5 py-4 font-bold text-slate-950">Diagnostic</td><td className="px-5 py-4 text-slate-700">Explains the cause</td><td className="px-5 py-4 text-slate-700">Win rate by segment, churn reason, stage ageing</td></tr>
      <tr><td className="px-5 py-4 font-bold text-slate-950">Forward-looking</td><td className="px-5 py-4 text-slate-700">Signals future risk or opportunity</td><td className="px-5 py-4 text-slate-700">Pipeline coverage, renewal risk, adoption depth</td></tr>
      <tr><td className="px-5 py-4 font-bold text-slate-950">Behavioural</td><td className="px-5 py-4 text-slate-700">Tracks whether the right process is followed</td><td className="px-5 py-4 text-slate-700">CRM hygiene, overdue actions, review cadence</td></tr>
      <tr><td className="px-5 py-4 font-bold text-slate-950">Outcome</td><td className="px-5 py-4 text-slate-700">Measures whether the initiative worked</td><td className="px-5 py-4 text-slate-700">Margin improvement, retention improvement, forecast accuracy</td></tr>
    </tbody>
  </table>
</div>

A weak dashboard stops at descriptive KPIs. A strong dashboard connects status, cause, and future action.

For example, in customer support, “average ticket resolution time” is descriptive. It tells you how quickly tickets are being closed. Used alone, it may create the wrong behaviour. Support teams may rush to close tickets quickly, even if the issue is not properly resolved.

<div className="my-8 rounded-2xl border border-teal-100 bg-teal-50/40 p-5 text-base font-semibold leading-8 text-slate-900 shadow-sm">
  Better pairing: Ticket speed + reopen rate. Ticket speed measures efficiency. Reopen rate protects quality.
</div>

This gives a more balanced view. The business is not just asking, “Are we fast?” It is also asking, “Are we solving the problem properly?”

## 5. Use KPI pairings to avoid narrow decision-making

Many KPIs become risky when viewed alone. That does not mean they are bad metrics. It means they need context. Some of the most useful KPI designs come from pairing a performance metric with a guardrail metric.

      <tr><td className="px-5 py-4 font-bold text-slate-950">Lead volume</td><td className="px-5 py-4 text-slate-700">Conversion quality</td><td className="px-5 py-4 text-slate-700">Marketing should create useful demand, not just activity</td></tr>
      <tr><td className="px-5 py-4 font-bold text-slate-950">Utilisation</td><td className="px-5 py-4 text-slate-700">Project margin</td><td className="px-5 py-4 text-slate-700">Busy teams should still deliver profitable work</td></tr>
      <tr><td className="px-5 py-4 font-bold text-slate-950">Ticket speed</td><td className="px-5 py-4 text-slate-700">Reopen rate</td><td className="px-5 py-4 text-slate-700">Fast support should not reduce resolution quality</td></tr>
      <tr><td className="px-5 py-4 font-bold text-slate-950">Revenue growth</td><td className="px-5 py-4 text-slate-700">Churn</td><td className="px-5 py-4 text-slate-700">New revenue should not hide customer leakage</td></tr>
    </tbody>
  </table>
</div>

This is where KPI design becomes more holistic.

For example, utilisation tells you whether delivery teams are being used effectively. But high utilisation on its own can be misleading.

A consulting or professional services team may have high utilisation but poor margin because work is being delivered inefficiently, projects are over-serviced, or senior resources are being used on low-value tasks. Project margin adds the commercial lens.

Together, utilisation and project margin create a better conversation:

- Are people busy?
- Is the work profitable?
- Are we using the right resource mix?
- Are we delivering within scope?
- Are we creating value without burning out the team?

That is much more useful than simply celebrating high utilisation.

## 6. Make trade-offs visible

Every business has trade-offs. Good KPI design should make them visible instead of hiding them.

    <thead className="bg-slate-50 text-xs uppercase tracking-[0.14em] text-slate-500">
      <tr>
        <th className="px-5 py-4">Trade-off</th>
        <th className="px-5 py-4">Why it matters</th>

      <tr><td className="px-5 py-4 font-bold text-slate-950">Growth vs margin</td><td className="px-5 py-4 text-slate-700">Fast growth can become expensive growth</td></tr>
      <tr><td className="px-5 py-4 font-bold text-slate-950">Speed vs quality</td><td className="px-5 py-4 text-slate-700">Faster delivery can create rework or customer dissatisfaction</td></tr>
      <tr><td className="px-5 py-4 font-bold text-slate-950">Utilisation vs employee burnout</td><td className="px-5 py-4 text-slate-700">Fully loaded teams may have no capacity for change or recovery</td></tr>
      <tr><td className="px-5 py-4 font-bold text-slate-950">Automation vs control</td><td className="px-5 py-4 text-slate-700">Automation improves efficiency but may reduce human oversight</td></tr>
      <tr><td className="px-5 py-4 font-bold text-slate-950">Volume vs conversion quality</td><td className="px-5 py-4 text-slate-700">More activity does not always mean better outcomes</td></tr>
      <tr><td className="px-5 py-4 font-bold text-slate-950">Forecast ambition vs forecast accuracy</td><td className="px-5 py-4 text-slate-700">An aggressive forecast may motivate action but reduce credibility</td></tr>
    </tbody>
  </table>
</div>

These trade-offs are where leadership judgment matters.

For example, a business may want to improve implementation speed. The obvious KPI is time to value or implementation cycle time. But if the business only rewards speed, teams may cut corners, skip discovery, reduce documentation, or push customers live before they are ready.

A better KPI design would include implementation speed, customer readiness, and post-go-live issues. This keeps the business focused on faster delivery without sacrificing quality.

Another example is automation. A finance or operations team may want to automate manual approval workflows. A simple KPI could be “manual hours saved.” That is useful, but incomplete.

If the process has compliance or financial risk, the KPI design should also include automation rate, exception accuracy, and control failure rate. This recognises the trade-off between efficiency and control. Automation is valuable, but only if the business can still trust the outcome.

## 7. Connect KPIs to incentives and behaviour

KPIs influence behaviour, especially when they are tied to performance reviews, bonuses, board reporting, or executive attention.

This is why a KPI should always be tested with a behavioural question: if people optimise for this metric, what might they do?

    </tbody>

This does not mean targets are bad. Targets are necessary. But they need to be designed carefully.

A KPI system should reward the outcome the business actually wants, not the easiest number to manipulate.

For example, sales teams may need a revenue target, but if the business is also trying to improve profitability, the KPI should not stop at bookings. It should also consider discounting, margin, deal quality, or customer fit. Otherwise, the business may win revenue today and inherit problems tomorrow.

## 8. Build KPI ownership, cadence, and action

A KPI is only useful if the business knows what to do with it. Before adding a KPI to a dashboard, it is worth asking a few design questions.

For example, NRR may be owned by Customer Success, but it is influenced by Sales, Product, Support, Professional Services, and Finance.

If Sales brings in poor-fit customers, Product adoption is weak, Support escalations are high, and onboarding is delayed, Customer Success alone cannot fix NRR.

That means the KPI needs clear ownership, but also shared accountability. The same applies to forecast accuracy. Sales may own the forecast, but Finance, RevOps, Marketing, and leadership all play a role in the forecasting process, pipeline standards, and review cadence.

## 9. Review KPIs as the business changes

KPIs should not be permanent. A metric that is useful during one stage of the business may become less useful later.

For example, during a CRM cleanup initiative, it may be important to track missing close dates, missing next steps, stale opportunities, incomplete account fields, and duplicate records. These are valuable operational KPIs while the business is fixing data discipline.

But once the process is stable, leadership may not need to review every data-quality metric weekly. Some can move into exception reporting or monthly governance checks.

The KPI lifecycle matters. A useful KPI today may become reporting noise tomorrow.

That is why KPI frameworks should be reviewed regularly, especially when the business changes direction, enters a new growth stage, launches a major initiative, or faces a new operating challenge.

## The right KPI changes the conversation

The best KPI is not always the most sophisticated one. It is the one that helps the business make a better decision.

A strong KPI framework should reflect:

- the direction of the business
- the current problems to solve
- the stage of maturity
- the decisions leaders need to make
- the behaviours the business wants to encourage
- the trade-offs that should not be ignored

That is why KPI design should move beyond isolated metrics.

Sales growth should be viewed with gross margin. Lead volume should be viewed with conversion quality. Utilisation should be viewed with project margin. Ticket speed should be viewed with reopen rate. Revenue growth should be viewed with churn.

  This is how KPIs become more than dashboard numbers: they shape focus, reveal risk, guide action, and improve the quality of business decisions.
